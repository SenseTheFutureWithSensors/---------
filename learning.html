Learning: Sensor Characteristics

Before wiring a sensor to an Arduino, ESP32, or industrial PLC, you must understand its physical and electrical characteristics. Learn the mathematical definitions, practical implications, and trade-offs of static accuracy, resolution, dynamic response time, frequency bandwidth, and calibration drift.

01 — Steady-State Performance

Static Sensor
Characteristics.

Static characteristics describe how a sensor behaves when the physical measurand is constant or changes very slowly over time.

Range & Span

Input Limits
Span = Xmax - Xmin

Range defines the minimum and maximum physical input values the sensor can measure without damage or saturation. Span is the algebraic difference between upper and lower limits.

Why It Matters Operating outside the range leads to non-linear clipping, severe measurement saturation, or sensor destruction.
Example: DHT22 Temp: -40°C to +80°C (Span: 120°C)

Sensitivity

Transfer Slope
S = ΔY / ΔX

The ratio of the change in electrical output signal ($\Delta Y$) to the change in physical input stimulus ($\Delta X$). Represents the gradient of the calibration curve.

Why It Matters High sensitivity allows detecting tiny physical changes, but increases susceptibility to ambient electrical noise.
Example: LM35 Temp Sensor = 10 mV / °C

Accuracy vs. Precision

Truth vs Repeatability
Err = |Ytrue - Ymeas|

Accuracy is how close a reading is to the true standard value. Precision (repeatability) is how consistently the sensor produces identical readings under repeated tests.

Why It Matters A sensor can be highly precise (repeatable) yet completely inaccurate due to uncalibrated zero-offset drift.
Example: DS18B20: ±0.5°C Accuracy over -10°C to +85°C

Resolution

Smallest Step
LSB = Vref / 2^N

The smallest increment of the physical quantity that can cause a discernible change in the output. In digital sensors, resolution is dictated by the internal ADC bit depth ($N$).

Why It Matters High resolution does NOT imply high accuracy; a noisy 24-bit ADC with poor layout has unusable lower bits.
Example: HX711 24-bit ADC = <0.001g weight steps

Linearity

Straight-Line Fit
NonLin = (Δmax / FSO)×100%

The degree to which the sensor's calibration curve conforms to an ideal straight line ($Y = mX + c$). Non-linear sensors require polynomial calibration curves or lookup tables in software.

Why It Matters Linear sensors (like RTDs) require minimal MCU processing; non-linear sensors (like NTC thermistors) require Steinhart-Hart math.
Example: PT100 RTD: >99.5% Linear over 0°C to 100°C

Hysteresis

Path Dependence
Hyst = |Yup - Ydown|

The maximum difference in output readings for a given input value when that point is approached from an increasing direction versus a decreasing direction.

Why It Matters Common in magnetic materials, mechanical diaphragms, and humidity sensors due to moisture absorption lag.
Example: Capacitive Humidity: ±1% RH Hysteresis

Dead Band & Threshold

Insensitive Zone
ΔX < Threshold → ΔY = 0

Threshold is the minimum physical input required to produce any initial output change from zero. Dead Band is the range of input values over which output remains completely unchanged.

Why It Matters Ultrasonic sensors have a blind zone (<2cm) where sound bounces back before the receiver electronics finish blanking.
Example: HC-SR04 Blind Spot: 0 cm to 2 cm

Drift & Zero Offset

Baseline Shift
Drift = dY / dt or dY / dT

Zero Offset is the non-zero output voltage when the measurand is zero. Drift is the gradual degradation of calibration over temperature shifts (thermal drift) or component aging over months.

Why It Matters Gas sensors (MQ-135) and MEMS gyroscopes require routine auto-zero baselining to cancel sensor drift over time.
Example: MPU6050 Gyro Drift: Requires fusion filter

Selectivity & Cross-Sensitivity

Interference Rejection
Y = S_a·A + S_b·B

The ability of a sensor to respond exclusively to the target physical measurand without being influenced by secondary environmental variables (such as ambient humidity, cross-gases, or electromagnetic fields).

Why It Matters MQ-2 smoke sensors react heavily to harmless cooking alcohol or sudden humidity spikes unless compensated.
Example: BME680: Gas sensor readings compensated by humidity
02 — Time-Varying Signals

Dynamic Sensor
Characteristics.

When physical inputs change rapidly (vibrations, sound, rapid temperature spikes), dynamic characteristics determine how faithfully the sensor tracks real-world transients.

Response & Rise Time

Transient Speed
tr = t(90%) - t(10%)

Response time is the duration required for the sensor output to transition from initial baseline to a specified percentage of final value following an abrupt step change in the measurand.

Why It Matters A bare thermocouple responds in <100ms; an industrial thermowell sheath adds thermal inertia, slowing response to >15 seconds.
Example: Bare bead K-type: tr < 0.1s in water flow

Time Constant (τ)

1st Order Exponential
Y(t) = Ymax(1 - e^-t/τ)

For a first-order dynamic system (like heat transfer in thermistors), $\tau$ is the time needed to reach $63.2\%$ of the total step change. After $3\tau$ the sensor reaches $95\%$, and after $5\tau$ it is settled ($99.3\%$).

Why It Matters Always wait at least $5\tau$ before taking calibration readings after introducing a sensor to a new temperature bath.
Example: NTC Thermistor: τ = 1.5s in stirred liquid

Bandwidth & Cutoff

Frequency Range
fc (-3dB Cutoff)

The range of input frequencies over which the sensor maintains faithful amplitude response without severe attenuation or phase distortion. The $-3\,\text{dB}$ point is where output power drops by $50\%$.

Why It Matters MEMS accelerometers used for motor bearing fault detection must possess high bandwidth (>5 kHz) to catch high-pitch vibrations.
Example: ADXL355 Accel: 1.5 kHz usable bandwidth

Damping & Resonance

2nd Order Oscillations
ζ = 0.707 (Optimal)

Second-order mechanical sensors (accelerometers, piezoelectric pressure transducers) have a natural frequency ($\omega_n$). Damping ratio ($\zeta$) prevents destructive mechanical ringing and overshoot.

Why It Matters Underdamped sensors ($\zeta < 1$) ring wildly near resonance; overdamped sensors ($\zeta > 1$) are sluggish. An optimal $\zeta \approx 0.7$ yields maximum flat bandwidth.
Example: Piezo Pressure: High resonance >100 kHz

Sampling Rate

Digital Conversion
fs > 2 · fmax (Nyquist)

How often the microcontroller samples the sensor output. The Nyquist theorem states sampling frequency ($f_s$) must be at least double the highest frequency component present in the signal to prevent aliasing.

Why It Matters Sampling a 100 Hz vibration at 150 Hz creates an artificial, destructive 50 Hz ghost phantom wave (aliasing).
Example: Audio Sampling: 44.1 kHz for 20 kHz audio

Settling Time

Final Equilibrium
ts (±2% Error Band)

The elapsed time required for the sensor output to enter and permanently remain within a specified tolerance error band (typically $\pm 1\%$ or $\pm 2\%$) of its ultimate steady-state value.

Why It Matters Reading an ADC too quickly after switching multiplexer channels gives corrupt readings because the internal sample-and-hold capacitor hasn't settled.
Example: ADC Multiplexer: 10µs settling requirement

05 — Hardware Design Rules

7 Sensor Engineering
Traps to Avoid.

Common mistakes in sensor interfacing, signal routing, and circuit design that ruin measurement accuracy.

1. Self-Heating in Thermistors

Passing too much excitation current through a thermistor causes Joule heating ($P = I^2 R$), artificially elevating its temperature above the surrounding air.

Engineering Fix Keep excitation current under $100\,\mu\text{A}$ or pulse current only right before ADC conversion.

2. Impedance Loading Errors

Connecting a high-impedance sensor (e.g. pH probe or piezoelectric crystal) directly to an unbuffered ADC drags down the signal voltage due to ADC input resistance.

Engineering Fix Place an ultra-high input impedance Op-Amp voltage follower (buffer) between sensor and MCU.

3. Ground Loops & ADC Noise

Sharing return ground wires between high-current inductive motors and sensitive analog sensors creates voltage drops that distort millivolt ADC readings.

Engineering Fix Use a star-ground topology with distinct Analog Ground (AGND) and Digital Ground (DGND) traces.

4. Aliasing High-Frequency Noise

Sampling high-frequency vibrations or 50Hz/60Hz AC mains electromagnetic hum without low-pass filtering folds the noise into the low-frequency spectrum.

Engineering Fix Implement an analog RC Anti-Aliasing Low-Pass Filter before the ADC at $f_{cutoff} = f_s / 2$.

5. Cross-Sensitivity in Gas Sensors

Assuming that a metal-oxide gas sensor (e.g. MQ-135) only measures CO2. In reality, changes in room humidity or ethanol vapors cause huge false positives.

Engineering Fix Measure ambient temperature and humidity in parallel, and apply compensatory matrix math in software.

6. Excessive I2C Cable Length

Running I2C sensor lines over long cables (>1 meter) creates parasitic bus capacitance, rounding off square clock pulses and causing bus lockups.

Engineering Fix Lower I2C clock to 100 kHz, decrease pull-up resistors to 2.2kΩ, or switch to RS485 or CAN bus.

7. Thermal Zero Shift in Load Cells

Strain gauge weighing scales drift by hundreds of grams over the course of the day due to thermal expansion in the aluminum beam.

Engineering Fix Use a full 4-arm Wheatstone bridge with temperature-matched dummy gauges and software tare recalibration.
